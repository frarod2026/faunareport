"Invasive species" is one of the few pieces of ecology that reliably makes the news, usually attached to a photo of something with teeth or a startling reproduction rate. It's also a term used far more loosely than the science behind it, and the looseness matters, because being labelled invasive can be a death sentence for a population.

## Three labels that are not the same

The confusion starts with treating three distinct ideas as one:

- **Non-native (or introduced).** A species living outside its historical range because humans moved it there, deliberately or not. Most non-native species are harmless and many are useful — a large share of the world's crops and garden plants are non-native everywhere they're grown.
- **Naturalised.** A non-native species that has established a self-sustaining wild population but isn't causing measurable harm. It simply lives there now.
- **Invasive.** A non-native species that spreads and causes significant ecological or economic damage — outcompeting natives, altering habitats, collapsing food webs.

Only the third category is "invasive". A great deal of public discussion applies the alarming word to species that are merely the first — present, foreign, and doing no harm.

## Harm is the hinge, and it's slippery

The definition rests on "harm", which sounds objective and isn't entirely. Harm to what, measured how, over what timescale? A species that disrupts an ecosystem in year one may be absorbed into a new equilibrium by year fifty. A predator that devastates native birds on one island may be harmless on the mainland it came from.

There's also a values question hidden inside the science. "Restoring" an ecosystem to a previous state is a choice about which past state counts as the correct one — and ecosystems have no single natural baseline, only a history of continuous change. Deciding that the arrangement from three hundred years ago is the right one, and everything since is contamination, is a defensible position, but it's a position, not a measurement.

## Why the label carries weight

None of this is an argument that invasive species aren't real or aren't destructive — some are among the leading drivers of extinction, especially on islands, where native species evolved without the predators or competitors suddenly introduced. Rats, cats, and a handful of others have genuinely erased species.

The point is narrower: because "invasive" licenses culling, eradication, and large public spending, the label should be earned by evidence of harm, not granted by foreignness alone. A species is not invasive because it arrived recently or looks out of place. It's invasive because it does damage, and that has to be shown, not assumed.

## How to read the word

When you see a species called invasive, it's worth separating the claim into its parts:

1. **Is it actually non-native**, or just locally unfamiliar?
2. **Is it established and spreading**, or a handful of individuals?
3. **What specific harm is documented** — and compared to what baseline?

The species that clear all three bars deserve the serious, often ruthless response invasive species get. The ones that clear only the first deserve a more honest label: they're simply new arrivals, and being new is not the same as being a threat.
