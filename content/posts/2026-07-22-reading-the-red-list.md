When a headline says a species is "Vulnerable" or "Endangered", it's quoting the Red List of the International Union for Conservation of Nature. It's the most widely used system in the world for classifying extinction risk. And it's read wrong most of the time.

The confusion starts with the assumption that the categories measure rarity. They don't. They measure **risk of disappearing**, which is a different thing — there are rare species that aren't at risk, and common species that are falling fast.

## The nine categories, from the end backwards

The system has nine categories. It's worth knowing them in the order that matters:

- **Extinct (EX).** No individuals remain. The last confirmed sighting of life has passed.
- **Extinct in the Wild (EW).** Survives only in captivity or in populations reintroduced outside its original range. It's a category of its own, and one of the most misunderstood — more on it in another article.
- **Critically Endangered (CR), Endangered (EN), Vulnerable (VU).** The three threatened categories. When a species is described as "threatened", it's one of these.
- **Near Threatened (NT).** Close to qualifying for a threatened category. The amber warning.
- **Least Concern (LC).** Assessed and judged to be at no significant risk — for now.
- **Data Deficient (DD).** There isn't enough information to classify it.
- **Not Evaluated (NE).** No one has assessed it against this system yet.

## The first mistake: treating "Least Concern" as "safe forever"

Least Concern means that, at the time of assessment, the species didn't meet the criteria for any threatened category. It's a snapshot, not a guarantee.

The date of the assessment is part of the information. A species classified fifteen years ago may have changed status several times since without the label being updated. Reading the category without reading the year is reading half of it.

## The second mistake: reading "Data Deficient" as good news

This is the most dangerous confusion. "Data Deficient" doesn't mean the species is fine. It means nobody knows.

Many Data Deficient species, once finally studied, land straight in a threatened category. The absence of alarm isn't the absence of a problem — it's the absence of anyone looking. For invertebrates, deep-sea fish and fungi, this category covers a huge slice of what exists.

## The third mistake: reading the category and ignoring the trend

Two species can both sit in "Vulnerable" and face opposite futures. One may be recovering and on its way off the list; the other may be sliding and about to move into "Endangered".

The Red List records that population trend — increasing, stable, decreasing or unknown — and it's often the single most useful thing on the whole assessment. The category tells you where the species is. The trend tells you where it's going.

## How to read an assessment in thirty seconds

Next time you see an animal's conservation status, read it in this order:

1. **The category** — the current risk.
2. **The assessment year** — if it's old, treat everything with caution.
3. **The population trend** — direction matters more than position.
4. **The rationale** — why this category, and on what quality of data.

A species that is "Endangered, assessed this year, population stable, with robust data" and a species that is "Least Concern, assessed twelve years ago, trend unknown" tell very different stories. Only the second line of each reveals it.

The system is good. It's the hurried reading that fails.
